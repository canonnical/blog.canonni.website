> “It's not computer literacy that we should be working on, but sort of human literacy. Computers have to become human-literate.”
> — *Nicholas Negroponte, founder and chairman emeritus of the MIT Media Lab.*

Human society has been one of continuous progress, ever since the advancement of the decoupled thumb. Nowadays, the average individual has the ability to initiate, and mold to their fancy, artificial constructs of life dependent on logical statements running on fragments of processed silicon. Apart from invention, civilization has developed another recurring theme: selection. The unfit perish, their able counterparts carry the torch forward, before a portion of them too are optimized in a likewise manner. These criteria of ability are fundamentally based on the grasp of technology and the adaptation of the new; the fire on said torch needed to be domesticated by a martyr, while lesser heroic men were charred into the ashes of history.

In the modern era, one in which men are more dedicated to the syndication of artificial fire, rather than the usage of such, the skills required for survival has greatly expanded. Despite the presence educational institutions available to most, as well as comprehensive knowledge receivable for free via the internet, lots remain, in some manner, behind on the blazing trail of progress. In near all cases, this does not correlate to incompetency, but rather reflects a flaw in our societal systems, which we must, with all power and might, remedy with tailored approaches.

The primitave, and most effective, way of learning is imitation of the teacher; pups hunt like their breeders, toddlers walk after watching their parents do likewise, and adults acquire skills by modeling themselves after the trainer. This approach works elegantly with common knowledge, such as grammar, algebra, swimming, and so on. The problem is clear, being that for niche or otherwise unusual fields of study, and with a lack of teacher proficient in such, it is near impossible for an ordinary individual to learn, in an orderly method, the relevant skills.

A res judicata would be the Rust programming language, whos usage in crucial public infrastructure, in a literal sense, guards humanity from extinction. This widespread adoption, somewhat counterintuitively, has not boosted its recognition in any significant form. On YouTube, the largest collection of audiovisual media, and a platform whose recommendation algorithm relies on the language, the popularity of tutorials on the Rust video game outrivals the language three times as much. This stark difference is caused not by the millions of Rustaceans worldwide, but rather the absense of approachable resources and creators of such.

This phenomenon can be roughly described as the “overgeekified community,” or one that is no short of subject-matter experts and adopters, but has no systemic program for the introduction and education of new members. As most programmers, and people interested in logical science by extension, have rather poor social skills, marketing of non-product digital technology has, since its creation, been a sparsely populated field.

As a contributor to its compiler and dependant subsystems, I have a strong conflict of interest writing this, but Rust is a marvel of human creation. It is neither functional or object-oriented, nor is it JIT-compiled or interpreted. The borrow checker enforces logical rules regarding memory ownership and mutability, while its flexible type system allows extensive modifications, all without a single error. Rust is, for lack of a better phrase, elegant as hell.

It is truly shameful to see only JavaScript, Python, C++, and other horrific monsters be taught in courses. They, instead of de-geekifing the programming language community, intimidate newcomers, who think all are like the flaming pile of cow dung they have just been presented. Rust, Go, Elixir, Haskell, and other novel languages deserve more exposition, not just by a group of Arch Linux-running geeks, but by the sane world.

Out with the old, in with the new! To quote Ray Bradbury, “There will come soft rains,” but before then, stay Rusty and code on, my fellow followers of the crab cult. Oh wait, what was I writing about? Ah yes, consumer technology…
    
## ...rant over.

Consumer technology only adds hay to the ever-burning pit, by being coated toys abstracted from their inner workings. In saturated markets populated by brands operated by subsidiaries of big tech, the ideal customer has minimum knowledge and maximum savings and purchases products solely based on sensation and not reason. By voluntarily acting as blind sheep, consumers are deprived of their fundamental right of choice and information.

Proponents of the devious system may hypocritically argue that internal components are irrelevant to ordinary usage; that a driver, for instance, need not know their engine’s configuration, and so on. This claim and likewise are fundamentally based on the false assumption that abstraction streamlines experience. To extend on the driver analogy, to be able to tweak the exact ratios of the machine, calculated based on comprehensive knowledge of its details, would be immensely beneficial to long-term usage, even with the initial hassle accounted for. When the optimal approach of providing out-of-the-box extensive options and a default profile is adopted by all, the average user, even when having no knowledge of the field, would be able to set sail smoothly and move its fine levers with ease. Optimum, in this and all cases, is hidden within a labyrinth by profiteers of the status quo.

Now, in the dysfunctional societies of our real unfortunate world, the sun will rise westward sooner than optimum is reality. The only feasible path for us ordinary yet conscious folk is, rather boringly, to think independently and examine all products with effort. The hopelessly idealistic ones among us, naively acting for the greater good, have unsuccessfully attempted to preach the gospels of openness to our peers. The blame lies on our communities, founded by the minority capable of interpersonal communication, and that are filled to their brims by geeks. Development is not exclusive with communication or publication, but man has a finite number of skill points, and the former being maxed results in depletion of the latter. The world is no short of experts of pieces of technology; millions are proficient in Linux Bash scripting, automobile engine building, or audio balancer equalizing; we simply lack a medium through which our moderate thoughts may be expressed and accepted by the masses.

To be frank, ultimately, the status quo will remain unchanged indefinitely; buyers don’t want to think, companies don’t want diminishing profits, geeks lack a reasonable voice, and products just work in their gilded casing. The sun still rises tomorrow, so sleep well and retain your mind for more important matters, instead of deciding what product to buy in a sea of distractions. To those geeky enough to know who David Hilbert was, remember the quote engraved on his grave, “We must know; we shall know; we do know.”
